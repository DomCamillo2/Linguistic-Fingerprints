## What is a Research Project?

 research is any structured process with the purpose of discovering new information
or conﬁrming and consolidating existing knowledge
 a project is often deﬁned as a self-contained piece of work with a clear endpoint
in the shape of a deliverable which needs to be completed within a limited time
 combining the two, a research project should have the following properties:
- work aimed at a deﬁned result that has not been reached before
- it should (potentially) lead to a new insight that other people might be interested in,
or help to consolidate previous ﬁndings on some question of interest
- scoped in such a way that it can be completed in the available time
- results in a report (paper or notebook) which other people can read in order to understand
your work and its contribution, also providing the possibility of building on your work

## Stages of a Research Project

 with a bit of idealisation (e.g. abstracting from the need to backtrack when things don’t work),
a research project can be conceptualised as going through the following stages:
- Stage 1: research question
- Stage 2: preliminary research
- Stage 3: formulating a hypothesis
- Stage 4: research design
- Stage 5: data collection
- Stage 6: data analysis and interpretation
- Stage 7: drawing conclusions and reporting ﬁndings
 performing all of these stages in full can be challenging for a student project,
so for each stage, we are going to see how the scope can be kept under control

---

## Stage 1: Research Question

 reaching clarity about what we want to ﬁnd out is the crucial ﬁrst step in embarking on a
research project of any scale: term project, bachelor thesis, master thesis, dissertation,...
 the ﬁrst step is to explore and delineate a research topic, which should be chosen based on
- familiarity with recent literature (for a student, this will come from a literature-based seminar)
- availability of resources and supervision (unfortunately very limited in this course)
- personal interest (a topic you connect with will make you more creative and productive)
 once you have selected a topic, the next step is to ﬁnd a worthwhile gap in current
understanding which could be closed by answering a research question
- the research question needs to be stated as clearly as possible
- it forms an important yardstick for deciding which points to pursue or drop
 important issues to reach clarity about at this stage:
- what is the nature of the problem addressed by the research question?
- what are the key variables involved in providing an answer?

## Stage 2: Preliminary Research

 survey current research surrounding your chosen subject area
- what is already known about the relevant variables?
- how is this area of research structured conceptually?
- are there any published views or results in the literature?
- are there any theories which would predict a certain answer to your question?
 understanding the wider background of the topics
 highlighting gaps in existing research
 if there is previous research, which datasets were used? are these datasets freely available?
 are there potential problems in the ways in which the data in previous research were
sampled? if there is a problem, what steps could be taken to ﬁx it?

---

## Stage 3: Formulating Hypotheses

 in order to answer your research question based on available data, you need to translate it
into testable hypotheses (educated guesses about the relationships between variables)
 each hypothesis must be possible to evaluate based on the datasets and computational tools
which are available to you (or which you can create within the timeframe of the project)
 well-formulated hypotheses will provide focus to your research,
and determine the directions to go into for data collection and analysis

---

<!-- page:8 source:DataScience_Lingo.pdf -->

## Stage 4: Research Design

 planning how to conduct the research in a way that achieves the objectives
and actually allows you to test the hypotheses
 investigate and consider the research methodology
 ethical and/or legal considerations connected to your data?
 how has it been used before in your ﬁeld of study?
 decide on sample size and sampling methods
 take stock of structure and size of available datasets
 research on tools and techniques for data collection
 design of the study (types of analyses, connection to testing the hypotheses)

---

## Stage 5: Data Collection

 reviewing records or existing databases
 digitalising / converting existing resources
 merging existing databases
 extracting new instances from corpora or the web
 overarching goal: enough data to be able to test your hypotheses!

## Stage 6: Data Analysis and Interpretation

 make good use of what you learned in this course!
 process and organise the data for analysis
 use statistical methods to test the hypotheses
 interpret results in context of research question and existing literature

## Stage 7: Drawing Conclusions and Reporting Findings

 communicate the results, document what you did in a reproducible manner
 discuss how results relate to the research question and the hypotheses you tested
 consider implications for theory and practice, possibilities for future research
 identify any limitations and caveats about your work,
suggest follow-up projects which might mitigate such issues

## Important Issues for Science Projects

 an important characteristic of a worthwhile and publishable research project is its novelty
(in the sense of creating some small amount of new knowledge about how the world works)
- the amount of research that is carried out all the time makes it difﬁcult to ensure this in
practice, it will typically take years to become familiar enough with the existing literature
(and any active researcher can only advise you on this for a very limited domain)
- student projects allow you to practise doing science without strictly requiring novelty
- there is a lot of value in replication, i.e. attempting to conﬁrm a result based on new data
 reproducibility is a huge issue, important measures to support it:
- always archive the unmodiﬁed original data (in case a resource disappears!)
- implement (or at least document!) all changes that you made to the original data
- use random seeds in order to make sure analyses can be re-run deterministically
- create README to at least document the steps needed to re-run your analyses
- in Python, it is crucial to always add a requirements ﬁle which lists all packages
that you worked with along with their version numbers
- always keep local backups (even in case everything is on GitHub, you might lose access!)

 linguistics is a huge ﬁeld, and there are many subﬁelds (e.g. sociolinguistics,
psycholinguistics) where I would not know what the current hot topics and open questions are
 this means that chances are very high you have attended courses that provide you with

 structured by the advanced modules in the General Linguistics BA, I can suggest some topics
connected to my own research which I would offer you in case you don’t have any idea already
 Variation, Evolution and Change: historical linguistics (language relationships, etymologies,

 Language and Cognition: lexical typology (polysemies as a reﬂection of universal concepts)
and second language acquisition (vocabulary learning and the role of extensive reading)
 Language Use: lexicography (patterns within deﬁnitions and translational equivalents)
and corpus linguistics (actual usage frequencies of collocations and rare constructions)

## Project Ideas: Variation, Evolution and Change

 Project 1: Can we decide whether languages are related by dating borrowed concepts?
- if languages appear related due to shared lexical features, this can be due either to
inheritance from a common ancestor or to extensive borrowing during language contact
- if we include the information how old concepts are, is it possible to decide based on the
distribution of shared words across time whether two languages are related by inheritance?
- we could build on this in order to pick apart the strength of shared inheritance and contact
for language pairs where both sources of similarity exist
 Project 2: Are languages with large free pronoun system less likely to drop pronouns?
- some languages have much more comprehensive systems of free pronouns than others
(examples: dual and paucal number, clusivity distinction, noun classes and gender)
- at the same time, many languages only use these free pronouns for emphasis, and allow
them to be dropped most of the time (the function being carried by verbal marking instead)
- it makes sense to think that languages only evolve large free pronoun systems if they are
functionally needed, so we would expect pro-drop to happen less in such languages
- question: can we show that there is a clear connection between these variables?

## Project Ideas: Language and Cognition

 Project 3: How robust are implicational universals of colexiﬁcation?
- colexiﬁcation means that two concepts are expressed by the same word in some languages
- colexiﬁcations are not independent (classic example: if a language does not distinguish
between hands and feet, it is not going to distinguish ﬁngers from toes either)
- we can extract many such potential implicational universals by investigating patterns in
databases of cross-linguistic polysemies, but can we be sure they are not random artifacts?
- use bootstrap resamples to get an impression of how robust such “universals” are
 Project 4: When should a learner shift from reading a single author to more variety?
- research on extensive reading, the key technique in developing broad written competence in
a second language, suggests that there is an ideal ratio of unknown to known words
- as soon as texts of full complexity become accessible, a common recommendation is to
stick to a single author in order to build up vocabulary while keeping new word density low
- at some point, the learner needs to transition to reading broadly, exposing themself to a
broad range of topics and authors in order to reach full competence
- how long should a learner stick to a single author in order to maintain the ideal ratio?

## Project Ideas: Language Use

 Project 5: Are deﬁnitions for basic concepts less basic?
- monolingual dictionaries specify the meaning(s) of words in terms of other words
- ideally, less common words are deﬁned in terms of more common words
- impression: there are some words (e.g. house) which are so basic that they need to be
deﬁned either very technically or in terms of less common synonyms or hyponyms
- question: can we substantiate this by showing that the average frequency of words used in
deﬁnitions of basic terms is signiﬁcantly higher than in those of less basic terms?
 Project 6: Are collocations analogous between languages?
- collocations (e.g. take an exam ) are a frequent type of word combination which are
characterised by an autosemantic base and a synsematic collocator
- while collocations can differ a lot between languages, one gets the impression that across
languages, the same activities tend to be expressed by collocations
- based on techniques for extracting collocations from parallel corpora, how often do
collocations correspond to collocations in other languages? does relatedness play a role?
- follow-up question: how well do the collocations match for certain language pairs?

## Project Proposal: Structure

 a complete project proposal should contain the following elements:
- concise and descriptive title, information about contributors
- an introduction laying out the research question,
providing the essential context and background information about the topic
- a clear statement of the objective: research question to be answered, result to replicate
- preliminary literature review showing that you have an idea of what is out there in terms of
both methods and datasets, and where to look for ideas and examples in case you get stuck
- considerations of scope: which aspects will be included in and excluded from the study, and
what are the reasons for the decisions? (most commonly: data availability, time constraints)
- proposed methodology: what are your plans for data acquisition, and which techniques are
you planning to apply to your data in order to answer the research question?
- preliminary project plan laying out the work each project contributor is planning to do in
which week, and how this means the work will be completed as the deadline approaches
- a statement of the expected outcomes: what will we able to learn from the project results?
what could we conclude from a failed replication or lack of evidence for your hypothesis?
